How this works

Nisab is the minimum wealth that makes zakat due. The Prophet ﷺ set it at 20 mithqal of gold or 200 dirham of silver, which works out to about 85 g of gold or 595 g of silver.

Because silver is much cheaper, the silver nisab is a far lower bar. Many scholars recommend it when your wealth is mostly cash, since more people end up paying and the poor benefit. Others say the gold nisab fits better today because it reflects real purchasing power. Pick one and use it every year.

Hawl is the year your wealth must stay above the nisab. Most people pick a fixed date, such as 1 Ramadan, and total everything they own on that day. The rate is 2.5% for a Hijri year. A Gregorian year is 11 days longer, so if you use one the rate scales up to about 2.577%.

How this works

Money is zakatable in full whatever account it sits in. Count the balance on your zakat date, not the average over the year.

Interest earned on conventional accounts is not yours to keep or count. Give it away to charity separately and leave it out here.

Crypto held as an investment or as money is treated like cash or trade goods by most contemporary scholars, so its full market value counts.

How this works

Gold and silver are zakatable in full at their melt value, not the shop price. Lower karats contain less gold: 22k is 91.7% pure, 21k is 87.5% and 18k is 75%. The calculator converts each weight to pure gold and multiplies by the 24k price.

Jewelry you wear is where scholars differ. The Hanafi school says it is zakatable. The Maliki, Shafi‘i and Hanbali schools say jewelry worn in normal amounts is exempt. Jewelry bought as a store of value, or kept and never worn, counts for everyone. Use the checkbox to follow the view you hold.

Gold ETFs and gold certificates represent metal you own, so their full market value counts.

4

Stocks

The rule depends on why you hold each share.

How this works

Shares you trade, meaning bought to sell for a profit within the year, are trade goods. Their full market value on your zakat date counts.

Shares you hold long term for dividends and growth are different. You own a slice of the company, and only the zakatable part of the company (its cash, receivables and inventory) counts. Buildings, machinery and other fixed assets don’t.

The exact way is to take the company’s zakatable assets per share from its balance sheet and multiply by your share count. Most people don’t have time for that, so many zakat bodies accept 25% of market value as an estimate. You can change the percentage on any row if you’ve done the exact work.

If the company pays zakat itself, as companies listed on Tadawul do through ZATCA, many scholars say long-term shareholders don’t pay again on those shares. Choose that option on the row. Trading shares still count in full because the reason you hold them is different.

Based on AAOIFI Shari‘ah Standard 35 (Zakah) and the resolutions of the International Islamic Fiqh Academy.

5

Funds and ETFs

Treated by what the fund holds.

How this works

A fund is a basket, so look through it to what it holds.

Equity funds and ETFs follow the stock rule. If you trade the units, count the full value. If you hold them long term, count the zakatable part, estimated at 25% unless the fund publishes a zakat figure per unit. Some Islamic funds do, and you should use it when they do.

Money market, murabaha and sukuk funds hold cash-like assets, so their full value counts.

REITs mostly hold buildings that earn rent. Only the cash they hold and the rent you received and still have counts, which is why the long-term estimate is the usual approach.

How this works

Zakat needs full ownership, meaning you can access and use the money. A state pension or an end-of-service benefit you can’t touch until you retire (GOSI, for example) isn’t zakatable yet. Most scholars say you pay on it once it reaches you.

For savings you could withdraw today, even with a penalty, count what would actually land in your account: the balance minus the early withdrawal penalty and tax. Then apply the stock or fund rule depending on how it is invested.

How this works

A loan to someone who will repay it is still your wealth, so it counts each year. A loan you doubt you’ll see again isn’t counted. If it does come back, many scholars say you pay one year of zakat on it then.

Anything you bought to resell, such as stock in a shop or land you plan to flip, is trade goods and counts at its current sale value. Your home, your car and land you plan to keep aren’t zakatable.

How this works

Money you owe and must pay soon isn’t really yours, so it is deducted. For long loans like a mortgage, deducting the whole balance would erase most people’s zakat while they live in a paid-for home, so the common approach is to deduct only the installments due in the coming 12 months.

Some scholars allow deducting only what is due right now. If you follow that view, leave the second box empty.

How the calculation works

The calculator adds up everything zakat applies to on your zakat date, takes off debts that are due soon, and compares the result with the nisab: the value of 85 g of gold or 595 g of silver at the price you enter. If your net wealth is at or above the nisab, zakat is 2.5% of it for a Hijri year, or 2.577% if you keep your zakat year on the Gregorian calendar, which is 11 days longer.

Cash, crypto, gold and silver bars, and anything bought to resell count in full. Gold is valued by its pure content, so 21k counts at 87.5%. Stocks and funds depend on why you hold them. Shares you trade count at full market value. For shares you hold long term, only the company’s zakatable assets (cash, receivables and inventory) count, estimated at 25% of market value unless you have the exact figure. Many scholars hold that long-term shareholders owe nothing more when the company pays zakat itself, as companies listed on Tadawul do through ZATCA. Retirement savings count only for the amount you could withdraw today, after penalties and tax.

Stock and fund rules draw on AAOIFI Shari‘ah Standard 35 (Zakah) and resolutions of the International Islamic Fiqh Academy. Rulings last reviewed November 2026. The calculator follows mainstream contemporary positions and says where scholars differ. It is a guide, not a fatwa, so ask a scholar you trust if your situation is unusual.
